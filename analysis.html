Author

Introduction

Traffic accidents remain a major public safety issue in the United States. This project investigates whether accident severity can be predicted using weather, roadway, and environmental conditions present at the time of an accident.

Research Question

Can accident severity be predicted using weather, roadway, and environmental conditions, and which factors are most strongly associated with severe crashes?

Severity level 2 accounts for the majority of reported accidents. This indicates that the target variable is highly imbalanced, so precision, recall, F1 score, balanced accuracy, and AUC are more informative than accuracy alone.

Overall, the weather variables vary quite a bit, but none of them show a clear difference between severe and non-severe accidents in these plots. Wind speed, visibility, and precipitation have some extreme values that should be cleaned before building prediction models. This suggests that weather alone may not be enough to explain accident severity and will likely work better when combined with road, traffic, and other environmental factors.

Weather and Roadway Features

Show code 
plot_severity_by_weather_group(accidents)

The proportion of severe accidents is fairly similar across the different weather groups. Rain, cloudy conditions, and the “Other” category have slightly higher proportions of severe accidents, while dust has the lowest. However, the differences are relatively small, suggesting that weather type alone does not strongly affect accident severity. These results indicate that weather is likely more useful when combined with other factors such as road conditions, traffic, and location.

Correlation and Missing Values

Show code 
plot_numeric_correlation(accidents)

The correlation heatmap shows that most of the weather variables have weak to moderate relationships with one another, with no pair showing a very strong correlation. Temperature and humidity have a moderate negative correlation, meaning higher temperatures are generally associated with lower humidity. Temperature and visibility have a moderate positive correlation, suggesting visibility tends to improve as temperature increases. Humidity also has a moderate negative correlation with visibility, indicating that more humid conditions are often associated with lower visibility. Overall, the relatively low correlations suggest that these variables provide different information and are unlikely to cause serious multicollinearity in the prediction models.

Conclusion

The model comparison should emphasize severe-accident recall, balanced accuracy, and AUC rather than overall accuracy because the response variable is highly imbalanced. Variable importance identifies features that were useful to the Random Forest, but it does not prove that those features directly cause severe accidents.
